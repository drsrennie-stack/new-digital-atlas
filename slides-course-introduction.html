How I chose this format

The evidence behind active learning

+6%

Higher exam scores

About half a letter grade higher on exams.

Active learning raised average exam performance by about 6 percentage points, which works out to roughly half a letter grade, across STEM disciplines.

1 in 3

Fewer students failing

Failure rate 33.8% → 21.8%.

Roughly one in three failures disappeared when courses added active learning, with failure rates falling from 33.8% to 21.8%.

Source: Freeman et al. (2014), Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Meta-analysis of 225 studies of undergraduate STEM courses.

There is great teaching in every format, and this is simply the approach with the strongest evidence for a course like ours. That is why I built our class time around it.

4

More instruction, not less

You still get the full lecture

Nothing has been cut. You get guided lecture in person all term, and on top of that you get the concept lecture videos online as your preparation. That is more instruction than a lecture-only course, not less, and the online half comes with some real advantages.

1

Watch on your terms

Pause. Rewind. Replay. Your speed.

Pause when you need a minute, rewind the part that went by too fast, and replay anything as many times as it takes. A live lecture moves at one speed. Yours moves at your speed.

2

Work in the order that helps you learn

Sheet first, video second. You control the order.

You control the sequence. Work the pre-work sheet with your notes and reading open first, then watch the video as your check. The lecture lands differently when you have already wrestled with the material.

3

Go back to exactly the concept you need

Jump to the concept you need. No rewatching everything.

The lectures are organized by topic, so when exam review comes you can jump straight to the one concept you are shaky on instead of listening to a whole recording again to find it.

So the lecture is all still here, and it stays here all term, on demand, whenever you need it back.

5

The order is on purpose

Why the worksheet comes before the video

The pre-work asks you to work the sheet first and watch the video second, and that order comes straight out of two lines of learning research, one for each kind of thing anatomy asks of you.

For ideas you can reason through

Struggle first, explanation second = deeper understanding.

What a plane actually cuts, how directional terms behave, why structures sit where they sit. Wrestling with problems before you hear the explanation builds deeper understanding than hearing the explanation first, and it carries over better to new problems.

Sinha & Kapur (2021), a meta-analysis of this exact question

For names you simply have to learn

Guessing before the answer makes names stick. Even wrong guesses.

There is nothing to reason out about why the styloid process is called that. But even guessing before you see the answer helps the name stick better than reading it cold. Researchers call this the pretesting effect, and it holds even when your guesses are wrong.

Richland, Kornell & Kao (2009); Journal of Cognition (2025)

Two instructions matter as much as the order. Keep your notes and reading open while you work the sheet, because supported effort is what works. And watch the video soon after you finish, because the video is your answer key, and answer keys help most while your attempt is still fresh.

6

How memory actually works

Your brain saves what it retrieves, not what it rereads

Feels productive, fades fast

Retrieve, don’t reread.

  • Rereading the chapter
  • Highlighting everything
  • Recopying your notes

Actually builds memory

  • Writing out what you know before you feel ready
  • Explaining a structure out loud from memory
  • Short practice sessions spaced over days

~50%

More remembered one week later

61% vs 40% recall one week later.

Students who practiced recalling material retained far more after a week than students who spent the same time restudying it (about 61% vs 40% recall).

Roediger & Karpicke (2006), Psychological Science; Cepeda et al. (2006) on spaced practice.

This is why the course is full of brain dumps, readiness quizzes, and daily recall cards. Each one gives your memory another round of practice at retrieving.

The method

Team-Based Learning: one cycle, four steps

1

Prepare

Sheet, then video. Before class.

Before class, you work through the pre-work sheet with your notes open, then watch the video to check yourself.

2

iRAT

Short quiz, alone. Your prep earns it.

The Individual Readiness Assurance Test, a short quiz you take on your own. Your preparation is what earns this score.

3

tRAT

Same quiz, with your team. Instant feedback. Argue it out.

The same quiz again, this time with your team and with instant feedback, so you can talk it through and make your case.

4

Apply

Real cases, together. Where anatomy sticks.

Your team works cases and application problems together. It is not graded, but it is where the anatomy really settles in.

We will do nine TBL sessions this term, worth 30% of your grade, with no make-ups or drops, so preparing becomes a habit. Notice that the whole cycle is built from retrieval, feedback, and application.

The rhythm

What a week feels like

Night before

Sheet → video → lab structures.

Work the pre-work sheet with your notes and reading open, watch the video to check yourself, and look over the lab structures.

Class day

Brain dump first (for points) → active learning → lab.

We open with a brain dump, a few minutes of writing down what you remember from the pre-work, and it counts for points. Then active learning, then lab.

TBL and exam days

iRAT → tRAT → cases. Exam days: practical + lecture exam.

On TBL days you take the iRAT, then the tRAT, then work cases together. On exam days you sit the lab practical and the lecture exam, one pair per module.

After class

A few minutes a day. Recall cards + Loops.

Spend a few minutes a day in Mastery OS with your Recall cards, and use the Loops videos to rehearse for lab.

The cycle is the curriculum. Keep the rhythm each week and the grade tends to follow, because anatomy builds on itself and steady practice beats cramming every time.

Your turn in this course

AI: encouraged for studying, strict for grades

For studying: encouraged

Quiz me. Explain it differently. Check my explanation.

  • Use the Teach it to an AI feature in Mastery OS to test whether you really have a concept
  • Have it quiz you, and answer before it shows you anything
  • Ask it to explain a concept a different way than the book

For graded work: strict

Only on marked assignments. Never on exams or graded TBL.

  • Generative AI only on assignments specifically marked as AI assignments
  • No AI on exams or on any graded part of a TBL
  • Be careful about trusting it blindly, because it can be confidently and fluently wrong

Here is a simple test for every use: when you finish, can you do it without the AI? If yes, it helped you learn. If not, it did the retrieving for you, and retrieval you skip is memory you never build.

When you are not sure where the line is, ask me. Asking is always the cheaper option.

Catch the AI

The fact-check drill

Step 1 · Ask

Ask an AI five anatomy questions.

As a team, ask an AI tool five factual anatomy questions. Start with body regions, directional terms, or anything you remember from prior classes.

Step 2 · Verify

Verify every answer against the source.

Check every answer against OpenStax or the course pages. Write down the section that confirms or contradicts each one.

Step 3 · Judge

Judge: correct, misleading, or wrong.

Grade each answer as fully correct, technically true but misleading, or wrong. Keep pushing into more precise territory until you catch one that is not quite right.

Step 4 · Report

Report your best catch.

Each team shares its best catch with the class: what the AI said, what the source says, and why the difference matters.

The point of this one is that verification is the real skill. In healthcare, an unverified answer becomes a patient safety problem, so we start the habit now.
